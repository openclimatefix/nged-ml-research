    Delivering uncertainty to NGED's power-flow studies

    National Grid Electricity Distribution (NGED) needs three deliverables for its power-flow studies: 11 stored quantiles per series and half-hour with a dependence table, a direct forecast of the meter of each bulk supply point (BSP), and a small set of unlabelled scenarios. The quantiles let NGED's own Python code rebuild marginal forecasts and coherent paths. A BSP is the substation that feeds a group of primary substations (Primaries). The direct forecast supplies the tail of the flow through a BSP, which a sum of node scenarios did not reproduce in any experiment we ran. This page collects the findings of experiments 434, 436, and 625 to 631 in one place. The page states the recommendation, five questions for NGED, and the corrections that later pages should carry. The results on scenario coupling rest on 10 calendar months of Tuesday runs for 16 Primaries, 3 wind farms, and 6 solar farms. The BSP results and the demand-tail results of experiments 629 to 631 rest on 5 development months, and the BSP results on 2 BSPs. NGED has not yet confirmed that any part of this design suits its workflow.

    Terms: K is the number of paths in a scenario set. A quantile such as q05, q95, or q99 is the forecast at the 5%, 95%, or 99% level. CRPS (continuous ranked probability score) scores one series' marginal forecast. The variogram score scores how well a scenario set's dependence between series and leads matches reality.

    The recommendation

    Store 11 quantiles per series and half-hour, and let the client rebuild paths from them. In experiment 628, 1,000 client draws from the 11 stored quantiles (0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.40, 0.50, 0.60, 0.75, 0.90, 0.95, 0.99) scored within 0.3% of the pipeline's 51 values on CRPS. The client extends the quantile function past q01 and q99 with a Gaussian tail and clamps wind to 0 to 100% of capacity. Solar is not clipped at 100%, because 2.3% of solar actuals exceed it. The client code is about 70 lines, and one run's 11 quantiles for 25 series take 772 kB.

    A Schaake shuffle helps the wind and solar farms and does nothing for the Primaries, so the dependence table is chosen by family. For the wind and solar farms, the table is 51 past observed paths, which a Schaake shuffle uses to rank-order independent draws. For the Primaries, the table is empty.

    • For the 3 wind farms and 6 solar farms, the Schaake shuffle scored 34.4% below independent draws on the variogram score (98.33% interval -42.5% to -26.1%, experiment 625).
    • For the 16 Primaries, no coupling we tried scored measurably better than independent draws. The fitted copula scored 0.7% lower (-1.5% to +0.2%) and a shuffle of anomaly paths 0.3% lower (-1.6% to +1.2%), and both intervals include zero. Coupling by the weather ensemble's member ranks scored 6.8% higher, which is worse. We therefore recommend no coupling for the Primaries.
    • A fitted Gaussian copula on its own scored 6.6% worse than the pipeline's version on the solar farms' variogram score. The likely reason is that the pipeline's version also uses the weather ensemble's member ranks, which the quantiles do not carry. We did not test this reason by ablation.

    About 20 paths score 2% to 3% worse than 51 paths on the farms' variogram score, and 20 paths passed the registered test only for the Primaries. In experiment 625, 20 paths scored 2.0% (wind farms) to 3.1% (solar farms) worse than 51 paths on the variogram score and 1.0% worse for the Primaries, and 10 paths scored 5.2% to 11.1% worse on the farms. The CRPS of 20 paths was within 0.5% of the 51-value CRPS in all three families. By the registered tests, the farms needed K = 51 and the Primaries K = 20, so "about 20" is a rounding of mixed results.

    Deliver a direct forecast of each BSP's own meter beside any node scenarios. Summing the Primaries beneath each of 2 BSPs (9 at Boston, 7 at Skegness) put 33.9% to 79.0% of actual export flows below the sum's q05 (nominal 5%) for the K1 demand network, in every coupling (experiment 629). Adding the 9 metered wind and solar farms and a time-of-day remainder reduced the export-side miss to 10.6% at Boston and 3.0% at Skegness for the Schaake shuffle, but the import side then had 14.3% and 32.6% of actual flows above the q95 (experiment 631). The direct forecast of each meter gave 4.9% and 7.2% below the q05 and 6.7% and 9.9% above the q95. Coupled sums did beat independent draws on CRPS by 2.6% to 7.7% once the farms were in. Coupling therefore still matters for the node scenarios.

    If NGED runs scenarios by hand, offer a choice of at least 10 paths, chosen without labels; a hand-picked 5 did not cover the tails. Experiment 627 found that ranking the 51 paths on one summary (for example the peak Primary demand), cutting them into K groups, and drawing one path per group beat a random draw on tail coverage in all 10 months. At K = 5 no rule kept the 5th and 95th percentiles of the chosen paths within 10% of the range of the 51 paths. For a BSP's export limit, an observed daily peak export exceeded the largest of 5 chosen paths on 12% of days (1.6% for all 51 paths) and the largest of 10 chosen paths on 6.3% of days. A label such as "the low-wind scenario" does not carry from day to day or lead to lead: under the Schaake shuffle a path's wind rank between days 1 to 3 and days 4 to 10 has a rank correlation of 0.19.

    The stored forecasts hold 11 quantile levels, but the production docs' delivery tables list 13. The 13 delivery levels (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.35, 0.50, 0.65, 0.80, 0.90, 0.95, 0.98, 0.99) share 7 levels with the 11 stored levels. Delivering all 13 means re-predicting the stored forecasts at the extra levels. Experiment 628 did not measure the effect of that re-prediction.

    Five questions for NGED

    The five items below are questions, not commitments. The answers decide which parts of the design are worth building.

    1. How many scenarios does NGED run by hand? In the advisor's illustration (an assumption, not NGED's statement), a hand-run study in a graphical tool allows about 3 to 10 runs per element per forecast, which sets how few paths the delivery must offer.
    2. How many power-flow runs can NGED afford, and are they scripted? A scripted study could allow thousands of runs, in which case NGED can draw its own scenarios from the stored quantiles. Related questions: which decision the study feeds (outage planning, flexibility procurement, or a curtailment notice), at what lead time, and what scalar each node takes (MW or MVA, which power factor, and power available or power after an active-network-management cap).
    3. Which BSPs, grid supply points (GSPs), or circuits matter? Can NGED give the weight each node carries in the flow through each constrained element, or link its electricity-network model to our series identifiers? Which nodes in the study do we not forecast, and what does NGED assume for them?
    4. Does a direct forecast of the flow through a BSP, GSP, or circuit already exist on NGED's side? We scored direct forecasts of the meters of 2 BSPs. The 2 GSPs have direct forecasts too, which we did not use in a sum.
    5. Should the delivery tables hold 11 or 13 quantile levels? The stored forecasts hold 11, and a move to 13 needs the forecasts re-predicted.

    What we tried

    • Experiment 434 (not supported). Re-ordering the stored demand forecast's 51 values by member temperature or by past observed paths did not beat independent draws across the 16 Primaries. Temperature ranks scored 6.7% worse than independent draws on the variogram score across the Primaries. The Schaake shuffle scored 3.5% lower (better) than independent draws on the variogram score across leads at days 1 to 10.
    • Experiment 436 (partly supported; joint block void, see the erratum on its page). Across the farms, a Schaake shuffle scored 28% (wind) and 40% (solar) below independent draws, and the copula-corrected ensemble ranks 29% and 43% below. Across the Primaries nothing beat independent draws by more than 0.7%. Tying scenario k to ensemble member k scored the same as a Schaake shuffle across Primaries and wind farms and no better across solar farms.
    • Experiment 625 (partly supported). Experiment 625 ran the K sweep and the anomaly-path shuffle described above. It also re-scored experiment 436's joint block and found no difference between couplings (within +0.8%).
    • Experiment 626 (partly supported). A separate upper and lower factor brought demand's q05 and q95 exceedance to 4% to 6% in every lead band, and did not fix wind or solar. Demand's q90 was still exceeded for 11% to 16% of actuals (nominal 10%), which points to a median that sits too low rather than tails that are too narrow.
    • Experiment 627 (partly supported). Experiment 627 gave the subset results described above.
    • Experiment 628 (partly supported). Experiment 628 tested the client-side rebuild described above. The tail rule (clamp, linear, or Gaussian) changed CRPS by under 0.02%, and the stored upper tails were too narrow for any of the three rules to repair.
    • Experiment 629 (not supported). Sums of the Primaries missed the BSP export tail. The threshold-weighted CRPS test could not rank the coupling methods, because neither BSP's meter reached the scaled export limit in the development months.
    • Experiment 630 (partly supported). The stored neural-network forecasts exceeded their q90 on 13% to 17% of event-free readings (5 development months). A correction fitted on the first three months removed about a quarter to a half of the excess on the last two, because the bias changes with the season.
    • Experiment 631 (not supported). Experiment 631 scored the farm-inclusive sums described above. The cause of the import-side miss (the Primary forecasts' bias, the farms, or the remainder) was not separated.

    Corrections to carry into other pages

    • Do not repeat that the 51 member tails are "7.6% to 8.8% too narrow" at q95. Experiment 436 took the q95 with numpy's default quantile definition. With the Weibull definition (the k-th smallest of 51 values sits at level k/52), the q95 exceedance is 5.2% for demand, 6.5% for wind, and 5.7% for solar (experiment 626). The stored upper quantiles of the demand forecasts are still too low, as experiments 626, 628, and 630 show.
    • Do not repeat experiment 436's joint-block verdict. Its scoring code mixed nodes and members, and experiment 625 replaced the joint-block scores. The within-family results of experiment 436 are unaffected.
    • Do not write that demand has no mixture. The bake-off's demand neural networks are mixture-density neural networks with 3 components, but the export stores only the mean of 5 seeds' quantiles, which is not any single mixture. The wind and solar mixture parameters were also never stored. Experiment 628 concluded that mixture parameters were not needed to rebuild marginal forecasts or a Schaake-shuffle coupling from the 11 quantiles. Recovering the parameters would need a rebuild of the inference of experiments 612 and 613, which has not been done.
    • Do not describe BSP sums as validated. Experiments 629 and 631 each rest on 2 BSPs and on a scaled export limit that was never reached, so the threshold-weighted CRPS could not rank the sums.

    Advisor review of the delivery design (2026-11-07)

    This section summarises an advisor review that read the background docs and experiments 430, 434, and 436 and proposed the delivery design above. The review is the fullest account we have of the reasoning for the design (the answer file is private to the research team). Statements about NGED's workflow that the review flagged as unsupported are marked (guess), and every statement about NGED's workflow needs NGED's confirmation.

    The binding quantity is the flow through one constrained element, and that flow is a weighted sum over many nodes. According to the background docs, for about half of NGED's generators the limit sits at a BSP or a GSP. For most of the rest it sits on a circuit and binds only after the next fault, found by power flow plus N-1 contingency analysis (one outage simulated at a time). In an almost radial part of the electricity network every weight is 1 for the nodes below the element. In a meshed 33 kV part the weights are power transfer distribution factors (the share of an injection at a node that flows through the element). The flow F through the element is then close to linear in the node injections: it is about the sum, over nodes i, of the weight w_i times the node's injection P_i.

    The review corrected four parts of the working understanding.

    • Reverse flow is a breach only where it exceeds the reverse-direction rating of a transformer or circuit, or pushes voltage or protection out of limits (guess about which binds most often in the trial area).
    • According to the background docs, real-time curtailment is done by active network management (ANM), not by a study days ahead. A study 1 to 10 days ahead is more plausibly for outage planning, flexibility procurement, or warning generators of likely curtailment (guess).
    • "A power-flow model takes only scalars" is true of one run and is not the constraint. A time-series power flow takes one scalar per node per half-hour. The real limit is how many runs the analyst can afford, which depends on whether the runs are scripted.
    • The power flow needs nodes we do not forecast: unmetered generators, other Primaries, and the sites metered in MVA (megavolt-amperes, which carry no flow direction; 8 of the 16 Primaries in experiment 629). Our scenarios cover only part of the sum.

    For curtailment, the tail of the sum decides the outcome, so dependence between nodes matters more than any single site's tail. The review's worked example is two neighbouring solar farms under one circuit with a 15 MW export limit, each forecast roughly Normal(6 MW, sd 2 MW) at noon (a simplification, because solar output is bounded by clear sky and capacity):

    Assumed correlation sd of the sum P(sum > 15 MW) q95 of the sum
    0 (independent draws) 2.83 MW 14.5% 16.7 MW
    0.62 (realised in experiment 436) 3.60 MW 20.3% 17.9 MW
    0.95 (ensemble copula coupling) 3.95 MW 22.4% 18.5 MW
    • Independent draws understate the chance of a breach by about a quarter and put the q95 1.2 MW too low. An overstated correlation puts the q95 0.6 MW too high, so generators are curtailed more than needed. The curtailment metric prices a breach at 20 times the curtailed energy, so overstating dependence is the safer error, but not a free one.
    • Experiment 436's finding that nothing beat independent draws across the Primaries does not mean the Primaries are independent. Their realised correlation was 0.29, so the sd of a sum of 16 equal Primaries is 2.3 times the independent value. Experiment 436's tail table showed 13.9% of actual group net flows above the q95 of independent-draw sums, against 6.6% for the Schaake shuffle and 2.6% for ensemble copula coupling. The variogram score barely moved. Experiment 436 also found the 51-value marginals too narrow in the upper tail, so these exceedances partly reflect the marginals. The review's conclusion is that the group-sum exceedance rate is the right instrument for this decision, and the variogram score is the wrong one.
    • Demand and solar errors are negatively correlated (-0.36 realised), so in the export direction a sunnier-than-forecast day also lowers demand through embedded solar and the two errors add. No experiment has tested cross-family dependence.
    • A Gaussian copula has no tail dependence. A Schaake shuffle copies real past days and keeps tail dependence empirically, limited by the size of its date pool, which favours the shuffle for the tail.

    No single delivery option wins. The review's comparison:

    Option Analyst's work Gets the tail right? Can we validate it?
    Many draws (marginals plus copula), analyst picks Moderate: sampling code, then choosing what "extreme" means Only as far as the marginal tails and the copula's tail dependence allow; 1,000 draws carry about +/-0.3 points of Monte Carlo error at q99 Indirectly, through group sums
    (a) Pre-chosen stress scenarios per element Least: feed K vectors straight into the power flow Yes at the chosen level, if the element flow's quantile is right The element quantile, yes; the split across nodes, no
    (b) Distribution of the element flow itself Low, but the result cannot be fed into a power flow Best, where the element is metered Yes, against the element's own meter
    (c) Importance sampling or conditional simulation High: weighted samples confuse non-statisticians Yes, efficiently Same as option (a)
    (d) Quantiles of group sums as extra series Low Yes for the named groups Yes
    • Option (b) has an advantage: the Boston and Skegness BSPs are metered and already among the series, so a direct forecast is trained and scored on the true flow with no copula. The review argued that a sum of node scenarios cannot match it, because unmetered load, losses, and the direction-blind MVA Primaries sit inside the BSP meter but outside any sum of our nodes. Experiments 629 and 631 found that sums of the Primaries missed the export tail of both BSPs, that adding the metered wind and solar farms removed most of the export-side miss at Skegness but widened the import-side miss, and that the direct forecast of each BSP meter came closest to nominal (within two binomial errors on three of four tails in experiment 631). Neither experiment tested the unmetered load, the losses, or the MVA Primaries separately.
    • Options (a) and (c) reduce to one recipe. For element E, day d, and level tau (for example 0.5, 0.9, 0.95, and 0.99 of that day's peak export), deliver the coherent vector of node values most likely given that the flow equals its tau quantile. Under a Gaussian copula and a linear flow this vector has a closed form, mu + Sigma w (q - mu_F) / sigma_F squared. With Schaake paths, the vector is the drawn path whose element flow lies nearest the quantile. The label "Skegness export, day 3, 95th percentile" names the target quantity and level, is recomputed in every run, and never refers to a member's identity, so nothing carries over from day to day.
    • A deterministic bounding case is worth adding beside the statistics: maximum generation with minimum demand. For solar, maximum generation is clear-sky output capped at capacity and needs no tail statistics. The added value is the probability that a day approaches that ceiling (guess that NGED's analyst already runs this case).

    The review recommended validating the element flow against its own meter on four quantities.

    • The exceedance rate at q90, q95, and q99 (and the 1%, 5%, and 10% levels on the export side), by lead band, with summer middays separately.
    • Threshold-weighted CRPS at the scaled limit (0.9 times the maximum export observed before the scored period). The score is proper and fits curtailment, because it scores every half-hour and weights only the region above the line.
    • The Brier score of the flow exceeding the scaled limit, with a reliability diagram.
    • The tier 1 curtailment energy proxy of studies.node_curtailment, with the realised unmet fraction.

    The independent sample is the weather day, not the half-hour, so q99 cannot be validated. Export-side tails live on roughly 150 to 180 summer half days a year per BSP (estimate). A q95 test then rests on about 8 to 9 exceedance days per BSP per year (binomial error about +/-1.6 points on a 5% rate), and a q99 test on about 2. The review said the firm limit had been reached once in the pilot, a statement we could not trace to experiments 629 or 631, which found the scaled limit never reached in the development months. The review listed what cannot be validated with our data:

    • q99 and beyond.
    • Tail dependence in the far tail.
    • Any element that is not metered, including every post-fault circuit.
    • Combined rare events, such as an outage on a clear-sky, low-demand day.
    • The split of a stress scenario across nodes, as distinct from the sum.

    The review advised stating these limits to NGED's analyst rather than covering them with a score.

    The review's recommended design. For each element NGED names, starting with the export side of the Boston and Skegness BSPs:

    • Deliver the element flow's quantiles from a direct forecast of the metered flow.
    • Deliver 3 to 5 conditional stress vectors per element per day, at tau in {0.5, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99} of the daily peak export, generated from Schaake paths and rescaled to hit the element quantile.
    • Keep per-node marginals (the review said 13 quantiles; the stored forecasts hold 11, see question 5) with widened tails, and the Schaake template dates, in Amazon S3 object storage, so a scripted analyst can draw more scenarios.

    The fallback is marginals plus a Schaake or copula table with client-side sampling, a 20-line client recipe, and published group-sum quantiles for the two BSPs so the analyst can check their own sums. The review's cheapest next experiment was a CPU-only re-score of experiment 436's stored scenario tables as BSP-group sums against the BSP meters. That proposal led to experiments 629 and 631, which used the daily-run forecasts rather than experiment 436's stored tables. The design with conditional stress vectors (the second bullet) has not been built or tested, and is the open question below.

    Open question

    No experiment yet tests how to make coherent node scenarios agree with the direct forecast of the flow through a BSP or GSP. NGED's power-flow study needs node injections, and the direct forecast supplies the flow through the element. Experiment 631 shows that the two disagree in the tails when the node scenarios are summed. One candidate design conditions the node scenarios on a quantile of the direct forecast. No experiment tests it yet, and we do not know whether NGED wants it. Questions 3 and 4 above bear on it.

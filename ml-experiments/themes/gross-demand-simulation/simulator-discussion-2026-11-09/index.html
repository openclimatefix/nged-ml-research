    The simulator discussion of 2026-11-09

    This page is a lightly edited transcript of a discussion session, not a reviewed result. Gross demand is demand before the embedded generation behind a substation is netted off. On 2026-11-09 the maintainer asked a Discuss session (an interactive Claude Code session used for discussion, which runs no experiments) whether a simulator of gross demand at substations would be useful, whether it could be built, and whether the simulator could itself be used as a forecast. The session answered from merged pages and stored outputs and ran no fits. The numbers in the record were not re-checked when the discussion took place. The table of checked numbers below says which ones were checked against merged pages on 2026-11-10.

    Take-home message

    The session judged that a simulator with exact labels would be most useful for evaluating forecasts. It judged labelling real data the cheaper first step. It also concluded that labelling alone cannot give the exact truth needed to score a forecast under two sets of rules. Those two sets of rules are the normal running arrangement (the electricity network as configured when no load has been transferred between substations) and the prevailing arrangement (the configuration in force on the day). The maintainer's starting point was that a forecast's score, and even the ranking of forecasting methods, depends on which set of rules is used, and the telemetry is not always in the normal running arrangement. A simulator that puts in every load transfer and every generator itself would know the correct labels for both sets of rules.

    What was asked.

    • Would a perfect simulator of net demand at substations, with controls for every input, be useful?
    • Is a good-enough simulator buildable, and with what methods?
    • Could the simulator, with its parameters inferred from real substation data, be used as an explainable forecast that can look months ahead and add a generator it has never seen?
    • How does heavy labelling of real data (load transfers, solar capacity, large generators, with hindsight and past weather) compare with building a simulator?

    What the session concluded.

    • Evaluation is the strongest use. Exact labels would let a forecast of the normal running arrangement and a forecast of the prevailing arrangement each be scored against its own truth.
    • Training data is the weakest use. In experiment 486, training with more artificial load-shift events raised forecast error on ordinary rows.
    • A first simulator would be assembled from parts the repository already has: the demand generalised additive model (GAM) with its calendar, temperature, heating, holiday, and clock-change terms; the physics solar and wind plant models; the injected-event generator; and the fitted per-substation properties of the substation cards. A generative model of the residual, a model of how often load transfers happen, and a fixed rule for when each battery charges and each engine runs would be new.
    • A bottom-up model of customers and appliances is not worth building first, because the session found no appliance, tariff, or behaviour data per substation in the project's files.
    • A simulator fitted to the metered record is circular when it is used to test methods that share its assumptions: the test then checks the simulator's own assumptions. The session proposed two defences: take the truth from independent sources (real metered farm output injected into a Primary), and judge the simulator by whether the existing forecasting methods keep their ranking on simulated series.
    • The simulator and a forward-model forecast would be the same code, run with chosen parameters or with inferred parameters. Its forecast skill is bounded by how well the hidden components can be separated from net power, and it would probably be less skilful than the current forecasts at short leads.
    • Labelling first, simulator later. Labelling has no realism gap and improves every score the project already computes. A peer session on graph approaches to load-transfer detection reported that its detectors find about two thirds of the megawatts in injected long load shifts, and name both substations for under a fifth of transfer ends. Those labels would therefore be biased towards large, long, one-sided events, and could not score forecasts under both sets of rules.

    What was left open.

    • The session did not establish which use the maintainer has in mind. Known-answer testing and scenario testing need different simulators.
    • The session proposed three cheap first experiments: a realness classifier on a first simulator, rank transfer of existing forecasting methods, and known-answer recovery of an injected farm. None was started. Their thresholds were the session's first guesses.
    • Which labels beyond load transfers a labeller should produce first. The session's view was reverse flow (power flowing from the Primary back up to the 33 kV network) at the eight MVA Primaries, which are metered in apparent power without a sign, then large-generator steps, then solar capacity.

    The maintainer then asked for an alternative to strict disaggregation to be assessed. Strict disaggregation splits every half-hour into the contribution of each asset. The alternative fits a list of assets and a few parameters per asset, and is assessed on An asset-inventory simulator.

    5. Session reply, 2026-11-09 14:28 UTC

    Answer: build a small, narrow simulator for known-answer tests only, and do not build a general one yet. A full simulator is worth it only if the first experiments below show that decomposition and forecast scores on simulated series track their scores on real series.

    What exists that a simulator could reuse:

    • Injected-event generator: experiment 660 (shared generator of injected load-transfer events) has no merged result.md. I found only its stored outputs: ev1 to ev4 JSON and parquet files in data/ml-experiments/00660-*. I found no realness-classifier result there. I did not read those outputs for this review.
    • Injected recall in those outputs: by event size, recall of injected events is about 6% for the 1 to 2 size bin and 23% for the 2 to 4 bin. On the real events in those bins the detector found 0 of 5 and 0 of 3.
    • Real-data injection harness: experiments 129, 157, 231, 238, 269 and 474 add a metered solar farm's recorded output to a Primary's reading and test whether the capacity fit finds it again. This is the nearest thing to a known-answer test we have, and it uses real farm shapes rather than a simulator.
    • Physics PV and wind plant models: the physics PV model (pv_physics) and the wind plant of experiment 347 already map weather to MW. They would be the generation half of a simulator.
    • Demand GAM terms: the convex demand model (dp_gam) has explicit heating, holiday, clock-change and Economy 7 terms, so it can generate a demand series from weather and calendar.
    • Demand networks and cards: the BG2 and K1 networks, the DP series and the substation cards give fitted per-substation properties to seed simulated substations.

    What the evidence says about the hardest parts:

    • Gross demand is not observed at the Primaries. The capacity fit recovers 0.75 to 0.88 of an injected farm (experiment 129).
    • Where the shortfall goes. Experiment 231 traced about 0.17 of the loss to the demand terms absorbing part of the solar signal and to the fixed solar shape not matching a farm's flat top. Experiment 269 found the plant shape explains the gain from using a nearby farm, and the sunshine does not.
    • Capacity estimates are not yet calibrated. Experiment 474 found no candidate shape passes its placebo test. The intervals are only honest with a cloud-load term, and experiment 129's coverage test was close to circular.
    • Neighbours do not help. Experiment 567 (neighbour lag encoders trained on synthetic shifts) was not supported: CRPS rose by 0.21 points of capacity. Experiment 459 found a donor-plus-recipient sum is not conserved.
    • Event labels are incomplete. Every count of real events is a lower bound, and "clean" rows may hold unlabelled shifts. Experiment 175 found about half of the worst unlabelled days are sharp-edged blocks that look like transfers.
    • Real data has features a simulator would need to reproduce. Examples are cloud-following load, soiling and meter-regime steps.

    Fitted model versus bottom-up simulator:

    • A fitted model of gross demand (GAM plus a residual model fitted to the metered record) is cheap and uses the tools we have. It is circular: a decomposition tested on it passes if it shares the GAM's assumptions. It is fit for testing code and for injection harnesses, and unfit for judging whether a method works on real data.
    • A bottom-up simulator (customers, appliances, heat pumps, electric vehicles, weather response) is not circular with our models. It needs data we do not hold: appliance stock, behaviour and tariff mix per substation. It costs months, and its realism cannot be checked against metered data it was not fitted to.
    • Recommendation: fitted for the first version, with truth injected from independent sources (real metered farm outputs, as experiments 129 to 474 already do). Bottom-up only for the scenario use.

      • Cost: about 2 CPU-hours. Stop if AUC is above 0.75; the simulator is then not real enough for any use.

    Argument against building one:

    • We already have partial known-answer tests. Injecting real metered-farm output into a Primary and re-finding it (experiments 129 to 474) gives truth without a simulator. Adding injected transfers (660) covers the second event class.
    • Cheaper first step. Add heating and load-shift injection to that harness, using the existing GAM terms, instead of generating whole series.
    • Training use is the weakest. Injected events have repeatedly cost accuracy on ordinary rows.
    • The binding limit is label quality, not data quantity. A simulator does not fix incomplete event labels, and it would inherit the GAM's blind spots.

    Questions for you:

    • Which use do you have in mind? Known-answer testing and scenario testing need quite different simulators.
    • Should I draft the plan for experiment 1 above? It needs your yes before the ML MANAGER session creates a worker.

    7. Session reply, 2026-11-09 14:35 UTC

    Short answer: yes to all three questions, in different degrees. A perfect simulator would be very useful for evaluation. A good-enough one is buildable in stages from parts we already have. A forecast built on it as a forward model is possible, but it is bounded by how well the hidden components can be inferred from net power.

    Q1: Would a perfect simulator be useful?

    • Evaluation is the strongest use. It fixes your "rules of the game" problem.
      • With perfect labels you can score a normal-running-arrangement forecast and a prevailing-conditions forecast against their own truths.
      • You can also see whether the ranking of algorithms changes between the two rule sets.
      • Real data cannot do this, because its event labels are incomplete and every real event count is a lower bound.
    • Known-answer tests come with it. Truth for PV, heating, load transfers, gas engines and batteries is known, so decompositions can be scored directly. Today we only have injected metered farms (experiments 129 to 474) and injected transfers (experiment 660).
    • Counterfactuals and scenarios (more EVs, heat pumps, PV) become possible, but only as far as the simulator's causal structure is right.
    • Training data is the weakest use. Artificial events have repeatedly cost accuracy on ordinary rows (experiments 486 and 567, the neighbour-lag encoders trained on synthetic load shifts). Realism, not volume, is the limit.

    Q2: Is it buildable, and with what?

    • The hard limit is that gross demand is never observed. Only the net is. At the Primaries the capacity fit recovers 0.75 to 0.88 of an injected farm (experiment 129). Part of the loss is demand terms absorbing solar; part is shape mismatch (experiment 231).
    • Components that exist now:
      • physics PV model and wind plant (experiment 347)
      • the demand GAM with heating, holiday, clock-change and Economy 7 terms
      • the gas-engine bands of experiment 264
      • per-substation fitted properties from the substation cards
      • the real-event injection generator (experiment 660, no merged page yet)
    • Components that do not exist:
      • a generative model of what is left over (the residual) that reproduces its size, autocorrelation and cloud-following load
      • an event process (how often transfers happen, how long, how large), learnt from labels that are incomplete
      • price-driven battery and generator dispatch
    • Technology I would consider, in this order:
      • GAM plus physics for the mean.
      • A conditional generative model (a neural network with a mixture-density output, or a flow or diffusion model) for the residual and for "samples" of real load blocks, as in your sampler idea. The samples would come from metered generators and from the cleanest Primary windows.
      • A hazard model for events.
      • Rule-based dispatch for batteries and gas engines.
    • Bottom-up customer and appliance modelling is not worth it first. We hold no appliance, tariff or behaviour data per substation, and it cannot be checked against metered data.
    • The circularity risk is real. A simulator built from our fitted models passes any test that shares their assumptions. Two defences:
      • Take truth from independent sources: real metered farm and wind output, injected.
      • Judge the simulator by rank transfer rather than realism alone. The question is whether our many existing arms (the GAM stack, BG2, XGBoost, lagged and lagless networks) keep their ranking and gaps on simulated series. The experiment log gives a large set of real rankings to compare against.
    • Other measures of realness: a realness classifier (as in experiment 660), the distribution of residuals, and the weather response.

    Q3: Could the simulator also be the forecast?

    • Possible, and the demand GAM stack already is a partial version. The research default is a physics-plus-GAM model with explicit terms. Extending it with generators, batteries and a learnt residual is a continuation.
    • What you would gain: explanation, forecasts months ahead, and a way to add a generator the model has never seen by giving it a plant type and capacity.
    • What you would likely lose: skill at short leads. At D8 the XGBoost model's lead comes from origin lags, and the GAM fixes did not close it.
    • Main obstacle: identifiability. Gross demand and unmetered PV cannot be fully separated from net power. The capacity placebo tests (experiment 474) failed for every candidate shape, and interval coverage needs a cloud-load term to be honest. Priors from the PV register and from metered shapes would have to carry the inference.
    • Useful for NGED? For new connections and long-range scenarios, yes. For day-ahead skill, only if it matches the best forecast on the tail and curtailment measures.
    • One object serves both uses. The simulator and the forward-model forecaster are the same model: run with chosen parameters it simulates, and run with inferred parameters it forecasts. Building it once for evaluation therefore does not waste the forecasting use.

    Short answer: heavy labelling of real data is cheaper and has no realism gap, but it cannot give true labels or control of the rules, so it solves evaluation only in part. I would do it first. It is also most of the work a simulator would need anyway.

    Where labelling wins

    • No realism gap. The series is real, so a score on it needs no argument that a simulator resembles the substation.
    • Cheaper and incremental. We already have detectors (the load-shift detectors, the lsc-detectors theme), the PV capacity table (experiment 474) and the gas-engine work (experiment 264). Each label set improves the evaluation by itself.
    • Hindsight helps. Labelling can use data from after the event, historical weather and neighbouring Primaries, which a forecast cannot.

    Where labelling falls short

    • The labels are estimates, not truth. Today's event labels are incomplete, and detector recall on real events is low in the bins I saw. Scoring a forecast against estimated labels bakes the detector's errors into the score.
    • It cannot twiddle the knobs. You cannot ask for twice as many transfers or a new generator. Scenario testing and counterfactuals stay out of reach.
    • Net power limits it. Gross demand and unmetered PV are not separable from net, so PV and demand labels carry the 0.75 to 0.88 recovery shortfall and the failed placebo tests.
    • Circularity moves, it does not vanish. Evaluating against labels made by a model scores a forecast partly against that model.
    • Rare events stay rare. Real data holds few labelled events (experiment 459 had 15 across the six), so event-level scores stay noisy.

    How the two compare

    Question Heavy labelling Simulator
    Ground truth for scoring Estimated, with detector error Exact, if the simulator is realistic
    Realism risk None Main risk
    Cost Lower, builds on existing work Higher
    Control of event counts and new generators None Full
    Fixes the rules-of-the-game problem Partly: labels let you define normal arrangement, but scores depend on label quality Fully, within the simulator's world
    Supports forecasting months ahead No Yes, as a forward model

    How they combine

    • Labelling is the first stage of a simulator. The event process, PV capacities, gas-engine behaviour and residual shapes the simulator needs are exactly what labelling estimates. Do the labelling, and the simulator becomes an assembly step.
    • A labelled series gives a cheap check on the simulator. Real labels give event rates and sizes for the simulator, and the classifier-style tests then compare the two.
    • A middle option: semi-synthetic evaluation. Keep the real series and inject known events and farms into it, as experiments 129 to 474 and 660 do. This gives exact truth for the injected part at no realism cost, and unlabelled real events stay as noise.

    My recommendation

    • Now: continue the labelling and injection route. It improves every score we already compute.
    • Later: build the simulator only if a question needs knobs, such as new connections or scenario months, which labels cannot answer.
    • Main risk of labelling alone: a score that depends on detector quality, so report label coverage beside every score.

    Short answer: a graph approach is plausible for labelling transfers, but the merged evidence is mixed. The graph experiments 651, 653 and 658 have no merged page yet, so I have not read their results.

    What the merged evidence says about the electricity network's structure:

    • Conservation fails as a hard constraint. In experiment 459, the sum over a donor and its labelled recipients still steps by about half of the donor's step. A detector that demands a zero-sum step across neighbours would miss or reject real events.
    • Neighbour values did not help a forecaster. In experiment 567, a neural network given its neighbours' lags and trained on synthetic transfers scored CRPS 0.21 points of capacity worse. Removing the neighbour lags at forecast time left its score unchanged, so it ignored them.
    • Neighbours do not explain unlabelled blocks. Experiment 175 found interconnected neighbours do not explain the sharp-edged blocks among the worst unlabelled days. Those blocks may be transfers to substations outside the 16 Primaries, which would hide the recipient.

    Why labelling is a better fit for a graph than forecasting:

    • Hindsight is allowed. A labeller can read neighbour data from both sides of the step, which a forecast cannot.
    • A step in one node and an opposite step in a neighbour is the signature. A graph model can test this directly, using NGED's interconnection table to say which pairs to compare.
    • Soft hints, not hard sums. This matches your earlier hierarchy aim: topology as a soft input, with a learnt tolerance for the half-sized leak seen in experiment 459.

    Limits to expect:

    • Recipients outside the 16 Primaries. The graph then shows a one-sided step. The detector must still call that a transfer, not a level change, using timing, shape and weather.
    • Incomplete labels as training truth. A graph model trained on them learns the labelling team's habits and misses the unlabelled events. Semi-supervised or injected-event training (as in experiment 660) would reduce this.
    • Few labelled events. With 15 real events across six Primaries in experiment 459, a flexible graph model could overfit. Injection helps, but injected events cost accuracy elsewhere in experiments 486 and 567.

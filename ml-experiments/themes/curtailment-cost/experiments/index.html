    Curtailment cost: experiments

    This page lists every experiment tried in the theme, newest first. An experiment that belongs mainly to another theme but bears on this one is marked "also relevant".

    Experiment Hypothesis Verdict Production status Take-home message Relevance
    00402 lag-gate-learned-weight-injected-load-shifts-part-1 A gate that sets the weight of a mixture of two XGBoost demand forecasts (one with the three power lags, one without) from the surprise of the lagged readings, trained only on injected load-shift events with known truth, beats the fitted constant mixture around real load shifts and costs no more than it outside them. partly supported not-for-production A gate trained only on injected load shifts, with no labels from National Grid Electricity Distribution (NGED), learns to lower the weight on the forecast with power lags from 0.51 to 0.42 when a lagged reading falls inside a load shift, and so removes most of the harm that the lags do in that case. The gain on real load shifts is small (0.10 points at most against the 50/50 mixture, and 0.04 or less against the constant mixture fitted on injected events), and the gate scores worse than the 50/50 mixture when the target itself is inside a real labelled event at the six Primaries. The fitted constant mixture per group is a simpler forecast than the gate and comes within about 1.4% of its exact CRPS on injected rows. Training the gate on real and injected events together is untested. also relevant
    00401 real-and-synthetic-load-shift-median-share-and-fixed-forecast-mixture Job 1. When a synthetic step of the corrected labelled depth is injected into clean periods at the same Primary and the same lag status as each real labelled event, the plain monthly control's median still follows the step by much less than it follows the real event, so the gap between 36.8% (experiment 395 Part E) and 0.94 (Part F) is not an artefact of the depth or the rows. Job 2. A fixed-weight mixture of the weekly forecast with power lags and the forecast without them scores within 0.1 points of effective capacity of experiment 400's learned blend B2 on rows with lags in an event, at the six Primaries with signed readings. partly supported not-for-production The median share of a load-shift step that experiment 395 called "followed" (0.94 with 3 lags in the event) is mostly not a response to the lags: a forecast with no power lags at all reaches 0.95, and the forecast with lags moves its median by at most about 0.03 more than the forecast without lags on the same rows, which is at the noise floor of two different fitted forecasts. Real events are far less visible to the lags than synthetic steps are, so the synthetic lag-encroachment sweep overstates what a real event does to the forecast. A fixed 50/50 mixture of the two forecasts does about as well as the learned blend at the six Primaries with signed readings, and not at the eight, where the interval allows it to score 0.12 points worse. At the six, one event at D16 carries most of the gain over the forecast with lags. also relevant
    00400 lagged-lagless-forecast-blend-around-load-shifts-stored A mixture of the weekly XGBoost forecast with power lags and the XGBoost forecast without power lags, weighted by a smooth function of the lags' surprise scores and of lead time, scores better than the forecast with lags on the rows whose lags touch a load-shift event, and costs no more than 0.02 points of effective capacity on rows outside events. not supported not-for-production The lag surprise scores from experiment 391 did not tell a blend when to trust the power lags: the fitted weight hardly moved around events, so every learned blend behaved like a mix of about 60% forecast with lags and 40% forecast without lags. A fixed 50/50 mix helps on event-lag rows at the six Primaries with signed readings and costs a little elsewhere, so a smooth blend driven by these scores is not worth porting. The next step is a learned blender trained on injected events, which supplies the event examples that the real rows are too few to give. also relevant
    00392 xgboost-nra-load-shift-masked-targets Removing load-shift events from the training targets of the monthly XGBoost demand model gives a forecast that stays near the normal running arrangement (NRA) level while a transfer is under way, and a mask built by the "deep and long" detector does so almost as well as a mask built from NGED's 66 hand-labelled transfers. Giving the unmasked model the surprise of its lags and of its neighbours (arm R5) gives the same NRA behaviour with no detector. not supported not-for-production Removing the labelled load-shift events from the XGBoost model's training targets does not make its forecast ignore a load shift in its lagged power inputs (the readings from the forecast origin, one day earlier, and one week earlier). When all three lagged inputs sit inside a synthetic load shift, the median forecast of the XGBoost model still moves by about 40% of the load shift's size, with or without the mask, and the band does not widen. Only dropping the lagged power inputs removes the effect, at a cost of about 0.7 percentage points of capacity in the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS). A mask built by an automatic load-shift detector (arm R2) was dropped and not tested. also relevant
    00383 load-shift-xgboost-oracle-first-part-1 On the rows whose power lags lie inside a hand-labelled load-shift event, the weekly XGBoost demand model's 5% to 95% band covers about 0.80 of the readings against 0.90 nominal. Nulling those lags (with 30% random lag dropout in training) or serving a lagless model on those rows, plus widening the band, brings the coverage within 0.03 of 0.90 and lowers the q95 and q99 pinball loss by 10% or more, without harming rows outside events. not supported not-for-production Telling the weekly XGBoost model which of its recent-power inputs lie inside a load-shift event does not fix its upper tail: nulling the inputs makes no measurable difference, and a lagless model helps at the six Primaries whose meters record the sign of the flow but hurts at the eight that do not. Fixed widening raises coverage on these rows but leaves it under the 0.90 target, and a factor fitted on earlier months reaches it only by doubling the band. The detector was not built, because even with perfect event labels no gain is established; the next experiment should try to widen the band by a learned amount on the flagged rows, or to forecast the transfers instead. also relevant
    00344 wind-research-default-1-speed-dependent-offsets Experiment 340 found that a constant upward shift of the wind quantiles gains 0.09 to 0.12 points on the kept rows but worsens the rows below cut-in by 0.51 to 0.78, and that the default's median is too low at 8 to 15 m/s and too high below 5 m/s. An offset that depends on the ensemble-mean 100 m wind speed at forecast time lifts the quantiles where the power curve is steep and leaves them alone where the forecast is near zero, so it keeps the gain without the low-wind harm. The starting point is Wind farms research default 1, the three metered wind farms W1 to W3 are scored, and the lead range is 1 to 10 days. not supported not-for-production Letting the wind forecast's quantile offsets depend on the forecast wind speed gains only about 0.02 points of capacity when fitted on the same retrospective forecasts (hindcast runs) that fit the quantile offset table, and the gain cannot be told from zero. Fitted on the six months just before the scored year (July 2025 to June 2026), the same speed-dependent correction gains 0.146 points and does not worsen the half-hours where the forecast wind speed is below the turbines' cut-in speed. The speed-dependent bias is therefore real, and it changes from year to year. Refitting the correction on recent months is the untested option that could capture it. also relevant
    00342 wind-research-default-1-upper-tail-offsets-and-head The upper quantiles of Wind farms research default 1 are too low: in experiment 324 the observed power exceeded q99 in 1.8% of half-hours (1% nominal) and q95 in 6.7% (5% nominal). Three corrections fitted on the hindcast can fix that without harming the headline score. x1 is a table of offsets for q90 to q99 that also depends on the ensemble-mean wind speed. x2 is a small boosted-tree head, fed the default's quantiles and summaries of the ensemble members' wind speed, that adds offsets to q90, q95, and q99. x3 lets the share of ensemble members above the farm's saturation speed set a probability mass at the farm's scale. not supported not-for-production The wind forecast's upper quantiles miss too often (observed power exceeds q99 in 1.8% of half-hours against 1% intended), and corrections fitted on past forecasts do not fix that: the offsets keyed by wind speed, the tail head, and the mass at the farm's cap all leave the share at 1.8% to 1.9% and change the upper-tail loss by about 1% or less. A head that sees the ensemble members does no better than one that sees only the default's quantiles, so in these 3 farms the members add no information about the upper tail. The remaining miss is consistent with the scored year differing from the hindcast years, which the next experiment could attack by refitting more often or by training on the most recent months. also relevant
    00340 wind-research-default-1-constant-shift-then-low-wind-drop Experiment 336 found that dropping the ERA5 training rows below the turbines' cut-in speed (x2) gains 0.117 points on the kept rows, and its audit traced about 85% of the gain to a constant upward shift of the quantiles. If that is right, a constant shift fitted on the 442-run hindcast (arm S) reproduces most of the gain without changing the training rows. Arm S+X2, which also drops the low-wind rows, then adds little beyond the shift. Wind farms research default 1 is the starting point, the three metered wind farms W1 to W3 are scored, and the lead range is 1 to 10 days. not supported not-for-production A single upward shift of the wind quantiles, fitted on past forecasts, does nothing, because the forecast's offset table already fits those runs. Moving the quantiles up by 0.8 to 1.2 points of capacity does reproduce most of the gain of dropping low-wind training rows, but it also worsens the forecast at low winds by 0.5 to 0.8 points, the same harm that stops X2 being adopted. An untested option that could lift the forecast at 8 to 15 m/s and leave it alone below 5 m/s is a correction that depends on wind speed. also relevant
    00339 wind-research-default-1-adapter-plus-mixture-head A three-component Gaussian mixture head on the hindcast, fed the quantiles of the physics core plus adapter and the ensemble members' summaries of speed, direction, and temperature (experiment 324's W4), lowers the quantile score of Wind farms research default 1 on the kept rows of the three metered wind farms W1 to W3 at 1 to 10 day leads. The head loses on the idle half-hours because it is trained on kept rows only, and the maintainer's view is that a forecast-time rule (predict the idle floor where the ensemble-mean wind speed is below cut-in) needs no idle detector. Three arms are tested: x1 the head as in experiment 324, x2 x1 plus the rule, x3 the head trained on all hindcast half-hours. partly supported candidate A small neural-network head that re-draws the physics-plus-adapter wind forecast's quantiles gains 0.27 points of capacity (2.4%) on the farms' normal half-hours and keeps that gain on the replicate months, but the same head given no weather summaries does as well, so the gain is a recalibration that a simpler correction partly reproduces. A simple rule that predicts the idle floor at low wind recovers none of the head's loss on the idle-band readings, and training the head on those readings removes the gain. also relevant
    00336 wind-research-default-1-below-cut-in-zeroing The maintainer proposes that, for wind, rows where the wind speed is below the turbines' cut-in speed carry only noise, so zeroing them lowers the quantile score of Wind farms research default 1 on the kept rows of the three metered wind farms W1 to W3 at 1 to 10 day leads. Three readings are tested, each added alone: x1 sets the forecast to zero power whenever the ensemble-mean 100 m speed is below the farm's cut-in speed, x2 drops the ERA5 training rows below cut-in from the XGBoost model and the offset table, and x3 sets the training target to zero on those rows. At most one is adopted, in the order x1, x2, x3. partly supported candidate Setting the wind forecast, or the training target, to zero below the turbines' cut-in speed does not improve the forecast, because the farms still produce small real output there. Leaving the low-wind rows out of training gains 0.12 points of capacity (about 1% of the default's score) on the kept rows, but mostly by lifting the quantiles upward, which a constant upward shift may reproduce (not yet tested). also relevant
    00335 primary-demand-default-3-tail-calibration-at-0-5-to-240-hours The 0.01, 0.05, 0.95, and 0.99 quantiles of Primary demand research default 3 miss their nominal rates past 96 hours (experiments 317 and 320 found 2.1% to 2.9% of observations below q01 and a 1.8% upper tail at 7 to 10 days). A calibration fitted per 24-hour lead band and per group (season, day or night, cold winter night) on the forecast errors of earlier origins brings the tails to nominal at every lead from 0.5 to 240 hours without worsening the quantile score. not supported not-for-production Primary demand research default 3 is already close to calibrated at 1 to 10 days: its 5% to 95% band covers 89.3% to 90.9% and 1.1% to 1.95% of observations fall below q01. Calibrations fitted on earlier months move the tail shares only part of the way, and none is worth adopting. A table fitted in-sample on the same year would fix the tails and slightly improve the score, which shows the table can describe that year but not that the miss repeats across years, so testing the miss on a second year is the next step. also relevant
    00332 lsc-curtailment-cost-of-label-free-mask Treating as missing the readings that a label-free load shift detector flags (arm A of experiment 327) lowers curtailment cost during and after load shifts, against no mask (arm C), without raising it on ordinary half-hours. not supported not-for-production Masking the load shifts that a label-free detector finds lowers curtailment cost by about a third when the forecast is judged against the normal running arrangement. Judged against the power the electricity network actually carried, which is what a curtailment instruction responds to, the mask changes cost by -3.0% with an interval from -24% to +30%, and it costs 1.3% more on ordinary half-hours. Adding the true moved megawatts to the true spans lowered cost during a shift, but adding them to the label-free mask did not, so this result cannot say what a live service needs. also relevant
    00327 lsc-label-free-detect-mask-train-pipeline A forecast of the normal running arrangement (NRA) can be trained and corrected on power with the load shifts set to missing, where a label-free detector finds the load shifts. The detector reads only how unusual the observed power is inside the predictive bands of a demand model that uses no lagged power, and no NGED switching label enters any threshold, mask, or training row. partly supported candidate A forecast of the normal running arrangement recovers faster after a load shift when the readings that a label-free detector flags are treated as missing: during and after injected shifts the 95% pinball loss falls from 1.79 to 1.04 points of capacity. On ordinary days the masked forecast is no worse, and slightly better. Without one site (D11) the gain shrinks to 0.30 points, and retraining the demand model on masked power adds nothing beyond masking the filter. also relevant
    00326 wind-research-default-1-change-arms Each of four changes, added alone to Wind farms research default 1, lowers the quantile score on the kept rows of the three metered wind farms W1 to W3 at 1 to 10 day leads: (a) 2 m temperature and an air-density column as XGBoost inputs, (b) hour of day and day of year as XGBoost inputs, (c) the ERA5-trained neural adapter's effective wind speed as an extra XGBoost column, and (d) one wind-speed scale factor, fitted on the hindcast to the implied wind, applied to every member's 100 m speed before the XGBoost model. At most one is adopted, in the order c, b, a, d. supported candidate Feeding XGBoost the neural adapter's corrected wind speed lowers the wind forecast's score by 0.10 points of capacity, about 0.9% of the default's score, at 1 to 10 day leads (99.75% interval -0.19 to -0.01). The change is negative at every farm and at short and long leads. The three other changes were not separable from zero. Only a port that puts the adapter in the production service can use the gain, so the maintainer decides. also relevant
    00321 metered-farm-lower-tail-recalibration-and-tail-head Metered solar farms research default 1 (the blend assembled by experiment 300, scored to 10 days by experiment 310) lets the actual output fall below its 0.01 quantile 3.3% to 3.7% of the time, and no mixture-density head tried in experiment 318 changes that. Default plus a recalibration table, a zero-inflated lower tail, or a tail-targeted head brings the share of actual values strictly below the 0.01 quantile close to 1% at leads of 1 to 10 days, without raising the quantile score and without losing experiment 318's 0.99 gain. partly supported candidate The solar-farm forecast's lowest quantile was already close to correct: only 1.2% to 1.4% of actual values fall strictly below the 0.01 quantile, because the earlier 3.3% counted the 3.0% of half-hours that read exactly zero. A recalibration table, a zero-inflated head, and a tail head each lower the own-history score by 0.04 points or less (the unshrunk table raises it by 0.03 to 0.05 points), so none is adopted. A shrunk recalibration table does lower the score by about 0.05 points for a farm with no history, and two heads lower the 0.99 pinball loss by 3.7% to 8.5% while covering the 0.99 quantile only 98.3% to 98.9% of the time. also relevant
    00312 lsc-how-good-must-a-detector-be-against-the-band-head A load shift detector that is far from perfect can still beat the best forecast that uses no detector, so a detector need not be near-perfect to be worth building. The one detector-based arm in experiment 297 that beat that forecast was the one that subtracts the true megawatts from the readings during a shift and adds them back to the forecast. This experiment degrades that arm one axis at a time to find where it stops beating the no-detector forecast. It also asks whether the surprise of recent readings against GAM_ONLY's residual bands (band position), or perfect knowledge of a shift, added as inputs to the no-detector quantile head, improves the head. This is question 3 of the LSC decision program, redefined by the Discuss ruling of 2026-10-23. not supported not-for-production A load shift detector does not need to be near-perfect to improve on the best forecast that uses no detector: with the true megawatts added back, finding half the shifts, learning of each up to 3 days late, or misjudging the megawatts by half still lowers curtailment cost against that forecast. The detector tested earlier (RO-NC) finds only a third of the shifts, below the break-even of one half (measured with every other property perfect). The surprise of recent readings against the forecast's bands (band position) found more, 37% of shifts on even months, but still under one half and with about 90% of its alarms false, so the next step is a detector that finds more shifts. also relevant
    00309 lsc-two-forecast-gap-choosing-without-truth NGED sees two forecasts and not the true switching state. A forecast with no lagged power (GAM_ONLY) forecasts the normal running arrangement, and a forecast with lags (K0) forecasts what will flow while an abnormal arrangement lasts. A rule that reads only the gap between the two forecasts, how long it has lasted, and a neighbour check can choose between them nearly as well as knowing the truth. This experiment is part 2 of the brief briefs/sw_lsc_two_forecasts_divergence_and_selection_brief.md, and it builds on experiment 308. not supported not-for-production NGED cannot pick between the two forecasts from the gap alone. A rule on the gap, or on the 24-hour mean of measured power minus the no-lag forecast, did no better than always using the no- lag forecast in the injected worlds, and worse than always using the forecast with lags in the real record. An oracle that knows the true switching state gained little, except in the 72 hours after a real shift ended, so the most promising signal to test next is a flag for the end of a shift. The gap is a filtered copy of the measured-power residual, so it adds no information that the measured power lacks. also relevant
    00304 metered-farm-node-sum-tail For the solar farms that share a bulk supply point, adding each farm's quantiles overstates the upper tail of the farms' summed output, adding independent bands understates it, and summing the 51 ensemble members' farm forecasts member by member gives calibrated 0.95 and 0.99 quantiles, because the ensemble carries the farms' shared dependence on cloud. not supported not-for-production The band for the combined output of the three farms beneath one substation comes out too wide if the weather forecast's 51 members are summed member by member, because the members' forecasts for the three farms move together almost perfectly while the farms' actual forecast errors are less closely linked. That band is almost the same as the band from adding each farm's quantiles. A band that assumes the farms' errors are unrelated is too narrow. A band from a Gaussian copula with a correlation fitted on the errors of the other 11 months comes closest to the stated probability. Only 5 half-hours in the scored year exceed 0.75 times a substation's maximum export, so this experiment cannot say what the band widths mean for curtailment cost. also relevant
    00297 lsc-need-a-detector-no-detector-arms-against-the-mask A forecast that never learns which readings are load shifts can match a forecast that uses a detector, because its bands and its recent-power correction can be made to tell a load shift from ordinary variation by themselves. This is question 2 of the LSC decision program. not supported not-for-production A forecast with no detector that recalibrates its bands and reads how far the recent-power correction has moved scores better than the RO-NC detector near load shifts, so with RO-NC a detector is not shown to be needed. Perfect knowledge with the true megawatts added back still lowers curtailment cost by 6.5% (3.9 to 9.2) against that forecast, so the next step is to run the better LSC detector (LR_b20) on the same simulated shifts and judge it against a forecast that has the recalibrated bands. also relevant
    00296 lsc-benefit-by-event-class-long-deep-versus-small-shifts Load shifts of at least 2 days and at least 10% of a Primary substation's load carry most of the benefit of knowing every load shift exactly, and shifts that are shorter than 12 hours or under 10% of load carry little. This is the question behind the "long-and-deep" detector of the LSC decision program. supported not-for-production A detector that finds only load shifts of at least 2 days and at least 10% of a substation's load would deliver about 96% of the upper-tail forecast benefit and about 90% of the curtailment-cost benefit of knowing every shift, if it were perfect. Detecting shifts from 6 hours to 2 days would add 3% to 8% more, and detecting shifts down to 5% of load would add 1% to 2%. The next step is to weight these shares by how often a real long-and-deep detector finds each class, which waits for Part 1 of the long-deep detector experiment. also relevant
    00288 pooled-head-on-public-wind-farms A neural head that adds 11 quantile offsets to the XGBoost wind forecast (R0) and was trained on dozens of public onshore wind farms (metered output from Elexon's Balancing Mechanism Reporting Service) lowers the quantile score of the three metered wind farms (W1 to W3) against R0 alone, and a head trained on the pool beats the same head trained on W1 to W3 only, because experiment 259's head had too few farm-years to learn a correction that transfers. A second hypothesis: XGBoost keeps improving as the training history grows from 12 to 48 months while the physics core is flat after 24 months, and a pool of public farms replaces the missing history. not supported not-for-production Training the correction on 40 public wind farms did not help the forecasts for the three NGED wind farms. Applied directly, it made them worse, because the public farms' capacity, turbine rating, and hub height let the head memorise each farm. Without those inputs it did no harm, and after a short fine-tune on the three farms' own data it gained about as much as a simple offset table fitted on those farms, so the public farms added nothing measurable. also relevant
    00284 metered-farm-tail-head-past-weather Experiment 275 found that adding years of past-weather rows lowered the 0.99 pinball loss of the neural head H5 by 4.6% while its overall score moved by 0.47%. A small neural network trained with a loss restricted to the tail quantiles (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.95, 0.98, 0.99), on the same past-weather plus ensemble rows with an era flag, lowers the 0.01 and 0.99 pinball loss of a metered solar farm's forecast below that of XGBoost trained on the same rows. partly supported not-for-production A neural network trained only to forecast the extreme edges of a solar farm's output band does beat XGBoost at the 0.99 quantile (the upper edge that matters for curtailment) by 30%, but the existing H5 network does slightly better still, and most of the gain comes from building the band around the physical power model and not from tail-only training. The years of past-weather rows add under 1% at the 0.99 quantile, and at the 0.01 quantile the share of half-hours below it is outside 0.5% to 1.5% at three farms (marginally at A, because of exact zeros at D, and clearly only at E). also relevant
    00283 lsc-perfect-knowledge-value-to-forecast-and-curtailment Knowing every load shift exactly (its true start and end) lowers the error of the forecast of the normal running arrangement (NRA) in the 48 hours after a shift, and lowers the curtailment cost of the forecast of observed power in the days around a shift, while leaving ordinary rows unchanged. This is question 1 of the LSC decision program. partly supported not-for-production Perfect knowledge of load shifts lowers the forecast's error most in the 48 hours after a shift ends (23% lower upper-tail score, 95% interval 9% to 39%) and by under 1% over a whole year, because shifts are rare. Curtailment cost against observed power falls by 8% (6% to 10%) near a shift only when the known shift's megawatts are added back to the forecast, and does not fall when recent readings are merely set aside. The next step is to ask how good a detector must be to keep that gain, using the megawatts-added-back arm as the target. also relevant
    00277 lsc-curtailment-metric-on-existing-forecasts The curtailment-cost metric of experiment 268 (studies.node_curtailment, sorted quantiles) ranks the four existing forecasts as the other metrics do, and can resolve the differences between them well enough to judge the load-shift questions of the decision program. supported not-for-production Over a year of ordinary days, the curtailment metric ranks the four existing forecasts the way the other scores do, and it can tell the GAM stack from BG2 (BG2 costs GBP 7.35 million more on the six Primaries at the 0.5 limit, a figure that ranks forecasts and is not money saved). Inside the windows around the 7 logged load transfers, the GAM stack cannot be separated from BG2, because those windows hold only 926 to 2,923 scored half-hours. Questions about load shifts need injected worlds with many more shifts, not the real record. primary
    00271 lsc-load-shifts-net-out-at-bulk-supply-points A load shift between two Primary substations under the same bulk supply point (BSP) leaves the power flow at the BSP unchanged, so it barely changes the curtailment cost at the BSP, and only a load shift that crosses from one BSP to the other changes the cost noticeably. The step 7 program brief for the load-shift (LSC) decision program asks this in order to know whether the money question can be asked at BSP level. partly supported not-for-production A load shift between two Primary substations under the same bulk supply point changes the curtailment cost at that bulk supply point by 0.2% at a random time and 0.7% just before days that would be curtailed (interval up to 1.08%), because the two Primaries' forecast errors partly cancel.

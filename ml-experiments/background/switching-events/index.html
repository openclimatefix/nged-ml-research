    Switching events

    A switching event moves part of a substation's load to a neighbouring substation for a while, and the experiments that detect, inject, or remove such events carry the label LSC (load shift screen). This page says what a switching event looks like in the time series, why the project wants to detect the large ones, and what the detector should output. The detector experiments are listed on the LSC detectors theme page.

    What a switching event is

    • A switching event switches a circuit from one substation to another. The recipient substation gains the circuit's load, and the donor substation loses it. The experiments ignore that a circuit carries its own daily shape, and treat the event as a transfer of a constant amount of power (unchecked).
    • A switching event is temporary. The two substations return to their old levels when the circuit is switched back. Events last from hours to months (unchecked).
    • Switching events can overlap. For example, 1 MW may be switched away from substation A from Monday to Friday, and a further 0.5 MW from Wednesday. The total switched power is then 1 MW, 1.5 MW, 1 MW, and 0 (unchecked).
    • A circuit can go to several recipients, and some recipients are not in the dataset. In those cases the detector sees no partner (exp 653 to 692; 15 Primaries, injected events; provisional). Of the 66 hand-filtered real transfers, 21 have no metered receiver (docs).

    How a clear event looks in the time series

    The clearest real event seen so far shows all five features below. Most events show fewer of them.

    • A temporary level shift that starts and ends on a single half-hour.
    • The shift moves day-time and night-time demand by (almost) the same amount. A shift of demand-shaped size, or one that appears only by day, points to something other than a switch.
    • The shift is mirrored at the partner substation, roughly equal and opposite, and the start and the end fall on the same half-hours at both substations.
    • Both substations return to their old levels afterwards.
    • The sum of the two substations does not change, so the bulk supply point (BSP) above them does not notice the event.

    What the detector should find

    • The project cares about deep and long events more than shallow and short ones. Demand noise hides small events, and the experiments do not expect to see them. An event under 4 noise units is found essentially never (exp 732; D8, simulated events; provisional).
    • Not every level shift is a switching event. Some substations have permanent level changes, for example a new connection or a meter change. The normal-running-arrangement (NRA) forecast must follow those shifts. The NRA forecast must ride through a switching event, which is a transfer of load to a neighbouring substation (unchecked).
    • The ideal output is, for each half-hour, a distribution over the power that switching events have added to or removed from the substation. The distribution must allow overlapping events and must be able to say "unknown" (unchecked).

    Why the project wants the events

    • Use 1: interpreting lagged power features. If a lagged power feature lies inside a switching event, the forecast must try to undo the event, to give an NRA forecast (unchecked).
    • Use 2: scoring the NRA forecast. The evaluation removes the half-hours inside switching events from the time series (unchecked).
    • Use 3: reporting events to NGED. NGED cannot easily extract the switch instructions from their operational systems, so NGED would like the project to detect switching events from the time series and report them (unchecked).
    • The measured gain of use 1 is small. Perfect knowledge of the events improved the forecast by about 1.5 pinball points around events and by about 0.005 over a year (exp 208, 212, 226 to 332; mixed designs; provisional). A band head with no detector matched a mask near shifts (exp 297; provisional).

    What labels exist

    • NGED's switching log is incomplete and noisy. It holds start, end, series, a power estimate, and a sign. The estimate and the sign are not reliable, and the log does not give the partner. Most logged events are far too small to see in the data (exp 580; 184 logged events; established).
    • The log shows 184 events, of which 23 last 7 days or more. Only 1 of those 23 appears as a step in the data (exp 580; provisional). The hand-filtered set holds 66 real transfers, of which 35 last over 1 day (docs).
    • No labels will exist for the scaled-up NGED area. A detector for that area must work without labels (unchecked).

    What the experiments found

    • Rule detectors find about half of the real transfers over 1 day. The rule RO-NC found 18 to 21 of 35 (exp 146, 302; real, one area; provisional). Recall on injected events overstated recall on real events (0.770 against 0.357; docs).
    • A neural network trained on synthetic transfers alone lost to the rule. It found 7 of 35 against 18 (exp 189; provisional). The 732 detector found 7.7% of the simulated steps of 5 points or more (exp 732; D8; provisional).
    • A one-day window hides the cues of a long event. The permanence cue needs more than 14 days (exp 714; 16 Primaries, injected bank; provisional), and a human sees the clear event only in a month of data.
    • The donor is the bottleneck. Pairing donor and recipient never passed 0.40 of the moved power (exp 665 to 692; injected events; provisional).
    • Training on real data is awkward because real data holds an unknown number of unlabelled events. An injected event lands on top of events that nobody has labelled (unchecked). A realistic simulator of substation demand with total control over events is a large piece of work that may be tackled later (unchecked).

    Ideas under discussion

    • A long-context neural network. A convolutional network would read about 6 months of the target substation and its neighbours, and output the switched-power distribution for the central month. It might also read the lag-free demand estimate, to remove seasonal signals, although that hurts as much as it helps at some substations (unchecked).
    • An unsupervised or weakly supervised detector that uses the cues that events are temporary and usually mirrored at a neighbour (unchecked).
